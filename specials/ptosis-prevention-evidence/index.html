Breast Ptosis Prevention — What the Evidence Says

A review of clinical evidence on Cooper's ligament protection, the role of supportive bras, and debunking common myths about breastfeeding and sagging.

What Is Breast Ptosis?

Breast ptosis (sagging) is defined clinically by the position of the nipple relative to the inframammary fold (IMF). In a non-ptotic breast, the nipple sits at or above the level of the IMF. As ptosis progresses, the nipple descends below the fold [1] . The Regnault classification system grades ptosis from Grade I (nipple at IMF level) to Grade III (nipple below IMF and pointing downward).

Risk Factors for Ptosis

Age and Hormonal Changes

Aging is the single most significant risk factor for breast ptosis. As women age, the collagen content and elastin quality of Cooper's ligaments decline, reducing their tensile strength. The loss of estrogen during menopause further accelerates ligament laxity and reduces breast glandular tissue volume [1] .

BMI and Weight Fluctuations

Multiple studies have identified higher BMI and significant weight fluctuations as independent risk factors for ptosis [2] . Each cycle of weight gain and loss stretches the breast skin and suspensory ligaments. A retrospective study of over 1,000 women found that smoking, higher BMI, and larger bra cup size were all significantly associated with increased ptosis severity.

Breastfeeding — The Evidence

"Large cohort studies have not found a statistically significant association between breastfeeding duration and breast ptosis severity after controlling for age and BMI." — Rinker et al., Aesthetic Surgery Journal (2010)

A widely cited 2010 study by Rinker and colleagues surveyed 132 women undergoing mastopexy (breast lift) and found that the "most powerful" predictors of ptosis were age, smoking history, higher BMI, and larger bra cup size [2] . Breastfeeding was not significantly associated with ptosis in the multivariable analysis. This contradicts the common cultural belief that breastfeeding itself causes sagging.

Can Bras Prevent Ptosis?

This is one of the most debated questions in breast health. The evidence is mixed, but the prevailing scientific consensus has evolved:

The Physiological Argument

Proponents of bra use for ptosis prevention argue that Cooper's ligaments, once overstretched, do not regain their original length. By providing external support and limiting breast motion, a well-fitted bra should theoretically reduce cumulative strain on these ligaments [1] . This is particularly relevant during exercise, where breast motion amplitudes are highest.

The Evidence

A 2013 French study by Rouillon et al. claimed that wearing a bra was "not a medical necessity" and that going braless could strengthen breast tissues [3] . However, this study had methodological limitations and its conclusions have been challenged by subsequent research. More robust biomechanical evidence consistently shows that breast support reduces ligament strain during physical activity [4] .

Current Consensus: While the long-term effect of bra use on ptosis prevention remains controversial, the biomechanical evidence strongly supports wearing a well-fitted, supportive bra during high-impact physical activity to protect breast connective tissue from acute strain injury.

Practical Prevention Strategies

  • Wear proper support during exercise: Use a high-impact encapsulation sports bra for running and jumping activities.
  • Maintain a stable weight: Avoid significant weight fluctuations that repeatedly stretch breast skin.
  • Do not smoke: Smoking degrades collagen and elastin, accelerating all forms of skin and ligament aging.
  • Get professionally fitted: An estimated 80% of women wear the wrong bra size, reducing the effectiveness of their support.
