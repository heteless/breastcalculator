What Is Accessory Breast Tissue?

Accessory breast tissue is a common congenital condition where extra breast tissue forms along the mammary ridge (also known as the milk line). The milk line extends from the armpit (axilla) down to the groin on both sides of the body. During normal embryonic development, the mammary ridge regresses except in the chest area — but when it does not fully regress, residual breast tissue can appear elsewhere.

The medical terms for this condition include polymastia (supernumerary breast tissue) and accessory mammary glands . When only a nipple or areola is present without glandular tissue, it is called polythelia (accessory nipple). Accessory breast tissue most commonly appears in the axilla (armpit area) , which is why it is often referred to as axillary breast tissue.

How Common Is Accessory Breast Tissue?

Accessory breast tissue is relatively common in the general population. Studies estimate the prevalence to be between 0.4% and 6% depending on the population studied and the diagnostic criteria used [1] . The condition is more frequently identified in women than in men, though it can affect both sexes.

Type Description Prevalence
Polymastia (complete accessory breast) Full breast tissue including glandular tissue, nipple, and areola ~1-2% of women
Polythelia (accessory nipple only) Extra nipple or areola without underlying glandular tissue ~2-6% of population
Axillary breast tissue (most common) Glandular tissue in the armpit area, often without a visible nipple Most common form

Key point: Accessory breast tissue is a benign (non-cancerous) condition. It is not harmful to your health, though it can cause discomfort, cosmetic concerns, or functional issues with bra fitting and arm movement.

Causes of Accessory Breast

The development of accessory breast tissue occurs during embryogenesis . Around the 6th week of fetal development, two ectodermal thickenings called the mammary ridges or milk lines form on the ventral surface of the embryo. In humans, these ridges regress except in the pectoral region where the breasts develop. Failure of complete regression can lead to accessory breast tissue at any point along the milk line [2] .

Factors associated with accessory breast tissue:

  • Genetics: Familial inheritance patterns have been observed. The condition follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern with variable penetrance in some families.
  • Embryological remnants: The primary cause is incomplete regression of the mammary ridge during embryonic development.
  • Hormonal influences: Accessory breast tissue responds to hormonal changes just like normal breast tissue. It may become more noticeable during puberty, pregnancy, breastfeeding, or menopause.
  • Weight changes: Significant weight gain can increase the visibility and size of accessory breast tissue, as it contains fat cells that enlarge with weight gain.

Symptoms and Signs

Accessory breast tissue can present with a variety of symptoms depending on its size, location, and hormonal activity:

  • Visible lump or bulge under the skin, most commonly in the armpit (axilla)
  • Swelling or tenderness that worsens before menstruation (due to hormonal sensitivity)
  • Nipple-like projection on the skin surface (in cases of polythelia)
  • Discomfort when wearing bras — the tissue may be compressed by the bra band or cup edge
  • Arm movement restriction when the tissue is particularly large
  • Pain or discomfort during physical activity or exercise
  • Lactation — in rare cases, accessory breast tissue containing glandular elements can produce milk during breastfeeding

Important: Any new lump in the breast or axillary area should be evaluated by a healthcare professional to rule out other conditions. While accessory breast tissue is benign, it can sometimes be mistaken for other masses such as lipomas, lymphadenopathy, or breast cancer.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis of accessory breast tissue typically involves a combination of clinical examination and imaging:

Clinical Examination

A healthcare provider will perform a physical examination of the affected area. The characteristic location along the milk line and the presence of associated structures (nipple, areola) can often suggest the diagnosis.

Imaging Studies

  • Ultrasound: The most common imaging modality. Ultrasound can distinguish accessory breast tissue from other masses such as lipomas, cysts, or lymph nodes. It typically shows echogenic glandular tissue similar to normal breast parenchyma.
  • Mammography: May be used to evaluate the tissue further, especially in women over 40 or when there are concerns about malignancy.
  • MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging): Reserved for complex cases or when there is diagnostic uncertainty.

Biopsy

If imaging findings are inconclusive or if there is suspicion of malignancy, a fine-needle aspiration or core needle biopsy may be performed to confirm the diagnosis.

Treatment Options

Treatment for accessory breast tissue is generally not medically necessary unless symptoms are significant. Options range from conservative management to surgical removal:

Conservative Management

  • Observation: If the tissue is asymptomatic and not causing cosmetic concern, no treatment is needed.
  • Supportive bras: Choosing bras with wider side panels, higher cup coverage, and soft fabric edges can reduce discomfort. Full-coverage bras and minimizer bras may help conceal the tissue.
  • Pain management: Over-the-counter anti-inflammatory medications can help with cyclical discomfort.
  • Weight management: Maintaining a stable weight can prevent enlargement of the tissue.

Surgical Removal

Surgical excision (removal) may be considered for symptomatic or cosmetically concerning accessory breast tissue. The procedure is called accessory breast tissue excision or axillary breast tissue removal:

  • Standard excision: The surgeon makes an incision in the axilla and removes the glandular tissue and any associated fat. The procedure is typically performed under local or general anesthesia.
  • Liposuction-assisted excision: In cases where the tissue is predominantly fatty, liposuction may be used in combination with excision to achieve a smooth contour [3] .
  • Recovery: Most patients return to normal activities within 1-2 weeks. Scarring is usually minimal and hidden in the natural skin crease of the armpit.

Consult a specialist: If you are considering surgical removal, consult with a board-certified plastic surgeon or breast surgeon who has experience with accessory breast tissue excision.

Accessory Breast Tissue and Cancer Risk

Accessory breast tissue contains the same types of cells as normal breast tissue. Therefore, it can develop the same benign and malignant conditions as the normal breast, including fibroadenomas , cysts , and potentially breast cancer . However, the risk of cancer developing in accessory breast tissue is very low [4] .

Women with accessory breast tissue should continue regular breast self-examinations and include the axillary area in their self-exam routine. Regular mammography screening should include visualization of the axilla when possible.

Living with Accessory Breast Tissue

For most people, accessory breast tissue is simply a normal variation of anatomy that does not require any intervention. Here are some practical tips for managing it comfortably:

  • Choose bras with wider side wings and higher side coverage to encompass the tissue comfortably without compression
  • Look for bras with soft, seamless edges to minimize irritation
  • Avoid underwire bras that dig into the axillary area
  • Consider sports bras or bralettes for everyday comfort
  • If accessory breast tissue causes discomfort during exercise, try high-support sports bras with wide straps and full coverage
  • Monitor the tissue for any changes in size, shape, or texture, just as you would with your breasts

Frequently Asked Questions

Can accessory breast tissue go away on its own?

Accessory breast tissue is a congenital anatomical variation and does not resolve spontaneously. However, its appearance may change with weight fluctuations and hormonal changes.

Is exercise effective for reducing axillary breast tissue?

Exercise cannot eliminate glandular breast tissue. While strengthening the pectoral muscles may improve overall chest appearance, the glandular component of accessory breast tissue will not be reduced through exercise alone.

Does wearing the wrong bra size cause accessory breast tissue?

No. Wearing the wrong bra size does not cause accessory breast tissue. However, an ill-fitting bra can make existing axillary breast tissue more visible or uncomfortable. Bras that are too small in the cup or have a tight band can push tissue outward, creating the appearance of bulging near the armpit.

Is accessory breast tissue surgery covered by insurance?

Insurance coverage varies. Removal may be covered if it is medically necessary (e.g., pain, discomfort, skin irritation). Cosmetic removal for aesthetic reasons is typically not covered.
